From Chaos to Executive Meetings: How Meeting Minutes Became My Career Shield

發表於 2026-02-26 分類於 Career Essay

Looking back at a chaotic period at my former company, Taiwan Mobile, we experienced a major internal software transition. The company decided to unceremoniously retire Skype and issued a “mandate” from executives requiring all employees to use our in-house messenger, M Plus, for internal communication.

I understand the vision that a great product “needs” its own employees to be “happy users”. However, the sudden sunsetting of Skype caused significant friction, especially since early versions of M+ lacked basic features like cloud recording. But it was exactly this chaos—where re-watching meeting recordings felt like a waste of time—that forced me to develop the habit of writing Meeting Minutes. Little did I know, this would become the most critical pivot in my early career.

1. Survival in Chaos: Blind Typing and Key Extraction

During the era when M+ was still immature, I developed a competitive advantage: Blind Typing Speed.

I made it a habit to record key points and decisions the moment I heard them. If I encountered an uncertain detail, I’d jot down keywords and immediately clarify with colleagues right after the meeting. Over time, I realized I rarely needed to review recordings to produce a comprehensive summary. This not only reinforced my memory but also saved me hours of post-meeting digestion.

2. My Minutes Framework: Making Accountability “Stick”

A powerful set of meeting minutes is never a transcript; it’s a contract. I structured mine into four rigorous sections:

  1. Follow-up on Previous Items: Ensuring every pending task has an update.
  2. Business Unit Progress Reports: Synchronizing the latest status across teams.
  3. Meeting Conclusions: Solidifying consensus to prevent “he-said-she-said” later.
  4. Action Items: Explicitly listing the Owner and Deadline.

Example: IT Group to track project [X] progress and provide feedback to [Y] Group by March 15th.

Once these minutes were distributed—whether to the A Team, the B Office, or the C Office—accountability was crystal clear. It served as the recap for the next meeting and, more importantly, as my ultimate leverage and self-defense mechanism in cross-departmental collaborations.

3. From a 2-Year Rookie to Hosting Executive Briefings

I never expected that a skill many dismiss as “busy work” would earn me such recognition.

My managers were deeply impressed by my ability to organize complex information and synthesize logic. This directly led to me—a young professional with only two years of experience—being appointed as the TPM for a large-scale project for the Ministry of Digital Affairs, and even hosting work briefings for Director-level executives.

When standing before high-level management, my documentation skills kept me grounded. I knew the “why” behind every decision and exactly who was responsible for what.

Minutes are Discipline, and Discipline is Leverage

Meeting minutes are not just for others; they are a manifestation of internal self-discipline. They protect you from finger-pointing in complex projects and ensure your organizational skills are visible to leadership.

A great software engineer doesn’t just manage code; they manage consensus.
